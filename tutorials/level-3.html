Composition

Letters become words. Words become lines. Lines become poetry. This is where calligraphy transforms from drawing to writing \u2014 and from writing to music.

Connecting Letters

Persian script is cursive by nature \u2014 most letters connect to those adjacent to them. Understanding how letters join is the bridge between knowing individual forms and writing words.

Connecting vs. Non-Connecting Letters

Six letters in Persian never connect to the letter that follows them. These are the "non-connecting" letters: alef (\u0627), dal (\u062F), zal (\u0630), re (\u0631), ze (\u0632), zhe (\u0698), and vav (\u0648). After writing one of these letters, you must lift the pen before beginning the next letter. All other letters connect forward.

Key Concept

In Nastaliq, connections are not mere bridges between letters. The connecting stroke (kashideh) is itself an expressive element. Its length, angle, and thickness convey rhythm and can be adjusted to balance a word's visual weight.

The Kashideh (Connecting Stroke)

The kashideh is the horizontal or diagonal stroke that connects one letter to the next. In Nastaliq, the kashideh follows the characteristic descending angle of the script \u2014 approximately 8\u201312\u00b0 from horizontal.

The length of the kashideh is not fixed. It can be extended or compressed to:

  • Balance the visual weight of a word
  • Justify a line of text to a consistent width
  • Create emphasis or dramatic pause
  • Accommodate the varying depths of descending letters

Word Structure

A Persian word is written as a series of connected and disconnected letter groups. Each group flows as a single stroke or set of strokes, separated by the non-connecting letters.

Letters flow together in connected groups, written right to left

Letter Stacking in Nastaliq

One of Nastaliq's defining features is vertical stacking. In certain combinations, letters sit above or below each other rather than side by side. This creates the script's characteristic density and visual richness.

Common stacking combinations include:

  • Lam-Alef (\u0644\u0627): These always combine into a special ligature, one of the most recognizable forms in Persian calligraphy.
  • Letters above final forms: When a connecting letter precedes a letter with a deep tail, the first letter may sit elevated.
  • Dense clusters: Three or more connecting letters may stack in a diagonal cascade.

Spacing & Rhythm

Good calligraphy has rhythm. The spaces between words, between lines, and within words must all work together to create a harmonious whole.

Between Words

The standard word spacing in Nastaliq is approximately 2\u20133 nuqtas. This space should be consistent throughout a line, though slight variations are acceptable when justifying text to a margin.

Between Lines

Line spacing must account for the ascending and descending elements of the script. In Nastaliq, the descending tails of one line must not collide with the ascending elements of the line below. The standard line spacing is 10\u201314 nuqtas from baseline to baseline.

Practical Tip

Rule your practice sheets with baseline guides spaced 12 nuqtas apart. Draw a second set of light guidelines 4 nuqtas above each baseline to mark the top of short letters like ba and ta. This creates a consistent frame for your writing.

Visual Balance

A well-composed line of calligraphy should have even "color" \u2014 a consistent density of ink across the line. If one section looks heavier than another, the spacing needs adjustment. This is an optical skill that develops with practice.

The Rhythm of Writing

Persian calligraphy has an inherent musicality. The alternation between thick downstrokes and thin upstrokes, between connected groups and spaces, creates a visual rhythm analogous to musical meter.

Traditional masters describe this in terms of:

  • Zarb (beat): The regular pulse of similar letter forms recurring through a line.
  • Nafas (breath): The natural pauses between word groups where the pen lifts.
  • Mowj (wave): The undulating quality of a line in Nastaliq as words cascade from upper-right to lower-left.

Your First Words

Begin with simple, short words that use letter groups you've already practiced. These words are traditionally used as beginner exercises:

\u0627\u0644\u0644\u0647 allah God Lam-lam-he connection, alef start
\u0639\u0634\u0642 eshq love Eyn initial, shin medial, qaf final

\u0646\u0648\u0631 nur light Non-connecting vav between letters

Exercises

  1. Word repetition: Choose one word from the table above. Write it 50 times on a single page. Compare the first and last \u2014 note your improvement.
  2. Connection chains: Write strings of connected letters (not real words) to practice the kashideh: \u0628\u0628\u0628\u0628, \u0633\u0633\u0633\u0633, etc.
  3. Spacing drill: Write a simple sentence, then rewrite it three times with slightly different word spacing. Which looks most balanced?
  4. Line practice: Rule guidelines and write a single line of poetry three times, aiming for consistent baseline, spacing, and rhythm.
  5. Visual weight: Write a line, then squint at it. Do you see even "color" across the line, or are there heavy and light patches?
