Foundations

مبانی

Before a single letter is written, the calligrapher must understand the tools, the body, and the fundamental geometry of the stroke.

The Tools of the Calligrapher

The Reed Pen (Qalam)

The qalam is the primary instrument of Persian calligraphy. Cut from dried reeds (Phragmites), the pen's nib is shaped at a precise angle that determines the character of every stroke. The quality of your writing begins with the quality of your cut.

Key Concept

The nib of the qalam is cut at an angle between 45° and 70° relative to the writing line, depending on the script. Nastaliq uses a steeper angle; Naskh uses a shallower one. The width of the cut nib becomes your fundamental unit of proportion.

A well-prepared qalam should have a smooth, even cut with a small slit running up the center of the nib to regulate ink flow. The slit should extend 5–10mm from the tip. Too short and the ink won't flow; too long and you lose control.

Ink

Traditional Persian calligraphy ink is made from lampblack (soot) mixed with gum arabic as a binder. The consistency should be smooth and flowing — not too thick (which causes blotting) and not too thin (which causes bleeding). Modern calligraphers often use high-quality sumi ink or specialized calligraphy inks as practical alternatives.

Practical Tip

For beginners, Yasutomo sumi ink or Winsor & Newton calligraphy ink provides consistent results while you develop your stroke technique. Reserve traditional ink-making for when you understand how ink viscosity affects your line.

Paper

The paper used for Persian calligraphy must be smooth enough that the qalam glides without catching, yet absorbent enough to hold a crisp line. Traditional papers are burnished with an agate stone or glass to achieve this surface.

For practice, use smooth Bristol board or marker paper. For final works, seek out handmade papers from Isfahan or use Arches hot-pressed watercolor paper as a Western alternative.

The Inkwell (Dawat)

The dawat (inkwell) traditionally contains a small piece of raw silk or cotton that acts as an ink reservoir and regulator. You dip the qalam into this material rather than directly into liquid ink — this gives you far more control over ink flow.

Posture & Position

Calligraphy is a physical practice. Your body position directly affects the quality of your line. The traditional position is seated on the floor with the paper resting on one knee, but a desk works equally well if set up correctly.

  • Back: Straight but not rigid. Tension in the body transfers to the pen.
  • Shoulders: Relaxed and level. Drop them deliberately before you begin.
  • Elbow: Free from the table. The arm should move from the shoulder for large strokes, from the wrist for small details.
  • Grip: Hold the qalam between thumb and index finger, with the middle finger supporting from below. The grip should be firm enough for control, light enough that the pen can breathe.
  • Paper angle: Slight tilt (15–20°) toward the writing hand.

Key Concept

The calligrapher's breath matters. Traditional masters practice controlled breathing while writing — exhaling slowly during a stroke, pausing between strokes to inhale. This creates both physical steadiness and meditative focus.

The Nuqta: Unit of Measurement

Every proportion in Persian calligraphy is measured in nuqtas (dots). A single nuqta is the rhomboid mark created by pressing the full width of the qalam nib onto the paper at a 45° angle. This diamond-shaped dot becomes your ruler.

The Nuqta System — Every letter is measured in dots

In Nastaliq, the letter alef (ا) is typically 5–7 nuqtas tall. The letter ba (ب) is about 1 nuqta tall with a horizontal stroke of 5–6 nuqtas wide. Every letter in every script has established proportions measured in this system.

The Fundamental Strokes

Before writing any letter, you must master the seven fundamental strokes. Every letter in the Persian alphabet is composed of combinations of these elements.

1. The Vertical Stroke (Kashideh-ye Amudi)

A straight downward pull. This forms the basis of alef, lam, and other vertical elements. Begin at the top with the full nib width, pull downward with steady pressure. The stroke should be perfectly straight — practice against a ruled line until it becomes natural.

2. The Horizontal Stroke (Kashideh-ye Ofoghi)

A rightward pull at the baseline. This forms connections between letters and the body of letters like ba, ta, and sa. Maintain consistent nib angle throughout.

3. The Curve (Khomidegi)

A smooth arc, curving from vertical to horizontal or vice versa. This is the defining element of Nastaliq's flowing character. The curve must be continuous — any hesitation shows as a wobble in the line.

The Four Fundamental Elements — Every letter is built from these

4. The Ascending Curve

A stroke that lifts from horizontal to diagonal or vertical. Found in letters like nun and the tails of many final forms.

5. The Descending Tail (Dombaaleh)

The elegant downward sweep below the baseline. This is one of Persian calligraphy's most distinctive features — the tails of letters like nun (ن), ya (ی), and the final forms of many letters drop below the line in graceful curves.

6. The Hook (Ghollaab)

A small curved flourish at the end of certain strokes. This appears at the end of letters like dal (د) and the beginning of letters like sin (س).

7. The Dot (Nuqta)

The distinguishing mark placed above or below certain letters. Though small, dots must be placed with precision — their position, size, and angle all carry meaning. A misplaced dot changes one letter to another.

First Exercises

Begin with these exercises. Each should be practiced for a minimum of 30 minutes before moving on. In traditional apprenticeship, students would spend weeks on these fundamentals before touching a letter.

  1. Lines of dots: Fill an entire line with evenly-spaced nuqtas. They should be identical in size, spacing, and angle. This trains your nib control.
  2. Vertical strokes: Draw rows of parallel vertical lines, each exactly 5 nuqtas tall. Check them against each other for consistency.
  3. Horizontal strokes: Draw rows of horizontal lines of identical length. Focus on maintaining a consistent nib angle.
  4. Curves: Practice smooth quarter-circles connecting vertical to horizontal. No wobbles, no flat spots.
  5. Combined forms: Draw rows of the letter alef (ا) — a simple vertical stroke — as your first letter exercise.

Master's Advice

Do not rush to write words. The great master Mir Emad Hassani spent three years on fundamentals before writing his first complete word. Patience is not an obstacle to mastery — it is the path itself.
