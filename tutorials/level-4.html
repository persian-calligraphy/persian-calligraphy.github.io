The Great Scripts

\u062E\u0637\u0648\u0637 \u0628\u0632\u0631\u06AF

Three distinct scripts, three centuries of refinement. Nastaliq, Naskh, and Shekasteh each embody a different philosophy of the written line.

The Three Pillars

Persian calligraphy encompasses many scripts, but three stand as its greatest achievements. Each has its own rules, its own masters, and its own expressive range. To study all three is to understand the full breadth of Persian calligraphic art.

Nastaliq: The Hanging Script

\u0646\u0633\u062A\u0639\u0644\u06CC\u0642

Nastaliq (nasta\u02BFl\u012Bq) is considered the quintessential Persian script and the greatest achievement of Persian calligraphic art. Its name derives from a combination of Naskh and Ta\u02BFliq \u2014 it emerged as a synthesis of these two earlier scripts in the 14th\u201315th century.

Historical Development

Tradition attributes the invention of Nastaliq to Mir Ali Tabrizi (d. c. 1410), though it likely developed more gradually. The script reached its highest expression in the work of Mir Emad Hassani (1554\u20131615), whose mastery became the standard against which all subsequent Nastaliq calligraphy is measured.

Other great masters of Nastaliq include:

  • Sultan Ali Mashhadi (1442\u20131520) \u2014 Refined the script's proportional system
  • Mir Emad Hassani (1554\u20131615) \u2014 Achieved the script's ultimate perfection
  • Mirza Gholamreza Esfahani (1829\u20131886) \u2014 Revived the tradition in the Qajar era
  • Choheltan Kabuli \u2014 Influential in Afghanistan's calligraphic tradition

Defining Rules

  • The diagonal baseline: Words descend from upper-right to lower-left at approximately 8\u201312\u00b0.
  • Steep pen angle: The qalam is held at 60\u201370\u00b0 to the writing line, creating the characteristic contrast between thick and thin strokes.
  • Vertical stacking: Letters within a word may stack vertically, creating dense, compact word forms.
  • Deep descenders: The tails of letters like nun, ya, and final forms plunge well below the baseline in elegant curves.
  • Rounded connections: The kashideh (connecting stroke) curves gently rather than running straight.

Key Concept

In Nastaliq, the last letter of each word sits on the baseline. Every letter before it sits progressively higher. This creates the "hanging" effect: words appear to cascade down from right to left, like a waterfall of ink.

Nastaliq Exercise: The Word "\u0639\u0634\u0642" (Love)

This word is a classic Nastaliq exercise because it contains three distinct letter forms: eyn (with its complex initial form), shin (medial, with three teeth), and qaf (final, with its deep descending tail).

  1. Begin with the initial eyn \u2014 a small, open crescent at the highest point of the word.
  2. Flow into the medial shin, descending along the diagonal. The three teeth should cascade downward.
  3. End with the final qaf, its tail sweeping below the baseline in a deep, confident curve.
  4. Add dots last: shin receives its three dots above.

Naskh: The Foundation

\u0646\u0633\u062E

Naskh is the oldest of the three scripts studied here and serves as the foundation for all others. It is the primary script used for Quranic text and formal documents. Its upright, balanced character makes it the most legible of the Persian scripts.

Historical Development

Naskh was systematized by Ibn Muqla (886\u2013940) and perfected by Ibn al-Bawwab (d. 1022). The script's proportional system \u2014 which Ibn Muqla formalized using the circle and dot as units of measurement \u2014 became the mathematical foundation for all subsequent Arabic-script calligraphy.

Defining Rules

  • Horizontal baseline: Unlike Nastaliq, Naskh sits firmly on a horizontal line.
  • Moderate pen angle: The qalam is held at 45\u201350\u00b0, creating a more even distribution of thick and thin.
  • Clear, open forms: Each letter is distinct and readable, with generous interior spaces.
  • Measured proportions: Strict adherence to the proportional system \u2014 Naskh is the most mathematical of the scripts.
  • Minimal decoration: The beauty of Naskh lies in its precision, not its flourish.

Practice Advice

Many masters recommend learning Naskh before Nastaliq, even though Nastaliq is the more "Persian" script. Naskh's discipline and strict proportional system build habits that make learning Nastaliq easier. The reverse is rarely true.

Shekasteh: The Broken Script

\u0634\u06A9\u0633\u062A\u0647

Shekasteh (literally "broken") is the most expressive and least constrained of the Persian scripts. Born from the practical need for faster writing, it evolved into an art form that prizes spontaneity, rhythm, and personal expression over strict adherence to rules.

Historical Development

Shekasteh emerged in the 17th century as a rapid variant of Nastaliq. Its development is associated with Morteza Qoli Khan Shamlou and was further refined by Darvish Abdol-Majid Taleqani (d. 1771), who elevated it from a practical shorthand to a high art.

Defining Characteristics

  • Simplified forms: Many letters are reduced to their essential strokes. Dots may be omitted or combined.
  • Extended connections: Letters flow into each other with minimal lifting of the pen.
  • Variable pen angle: The calligrapher freely adjusts the qalam angle within a single word.
  • Overlapping words: In dense compositions, words from one line may interweave with the line above or below.
  • Personal voice: More than any other script, Shekasteh rewards (and demands) individual expression.

Key Concept

Shekasteh is not "sloppy Nastaliq." It has its own rigorous internal logic. A master Shekasteh calligrapher has internalized the rules so deeply that they can break them with intention and grace. You must master Nastaliq before you can meaningfully practice Shekasteh.

Choosing Your Path

Most students should follow this sequence:

  1. Naskh first \u2014 for 6\u201312 months. This builds discipline and proportional awareness.
  2. Nastaliq second \u2014 for 2\u20135 years. This is the heart of Persian calligraphy and demands the most sustained practice.
  3. Shekasteh last \u2014 only after achieving proficiency in Nastaliq. Shekasteh without a Nastaliq foundation is just bad handwriting.

Exercises

  1. Script comparison: Write the word \u0628\u0633\u0645\u200C\u0627\u0644\u0644\u0647 in both Naskh and Nastaliq. Note how the same letters take different forms.
  2. Pen angle drill: Practice writing alef at 45\u00b0, 55\u00b0, 65\u00b0. See how the angle changes the character of the stroke.
  3. Baseline exercise: Write the same sentence on a horizontal baseline (Naskh) and then on a descending diagonal (Nastaliq). Feel the difference in hand movement.
  4. Master study: Find a reproduction of Mir Emad's Nastaliq and attempt to copy a single word. Compare your version to the master's. What do you notice?
  5. Speed variation: Write the same word slowly (5 minutes per word) and quickly (10 seconds). The slow version trains precision; compare it to your fast version to see where your weaknesses emerge.
